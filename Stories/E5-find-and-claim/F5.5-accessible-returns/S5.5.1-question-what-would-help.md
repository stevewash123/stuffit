# S5.5.1 Question: what would help people with disabilities?

**Feature:** F5.5 Returns that work for people with disabilities
**Phase:** open question (research); future, tentative, speculative
**Milestone:** none until answered
**Depends on:** none

This is a question story. It is done when the answers below are written down, ranked, and turned into stories or dropped.

## The situation
A person who uses a mobility scooter gets around the city and does most of their shopping in stores. Tight or cramped stores are awkward, standing in line is harder and uncomfortable, and anything that can be done online is a help. Returns are one of the hardest errands because they combine a line, a counter and a bag of stuff. Other disabilities have their own versions: low vision and receipts, deaf or hard-of-hearing users and phone-only claims lines, cognitive load and long forms, fatigue and deadlines.

## Questions to answer
1. **Which steps hurt most?** Getting into the store, the line, the counter, carrying the item, phone-only support, forms, deadlines that assume a quick trip. Ask users, do not guess.
2. **What alternatives exist and how reliable are they?** Mail return with a prepaid label, curbside or door pickup, carrier pickup from home, store-credit-only returns, return kiosks, phone or chat claims. Which stores offer which, and are they free?
3. **What does the law actually require?** The ADA requires stores to make reasonable modifications to policies unless it would fundamentally change the business; it does not require online returns. What can we say, in plain words, with sources?
4. **What can StuffIt know that others do not?** The seller's return method per channel (S3.2.6), people's experiences (S5.5.2), and the deadline clock. Could it warn "this return needs a store visit within 9 days" early enough to arrange help?
5. **What can we do in the app itself?** Larger text and tap targets, screen-reader and keyboard use, voice input for the chat (S5.4.19), reminders that name the easiest route, a draft message asking for an accommodation ("please take this return at the entrance").
6. **Who can help us test?** Recruit a few users with different needs before building. Accessibility testing is not a checklist.
7. **What should we avoid?** Pity framing, storing health information, assuming one kind of disability, claiming a store must do something it does not.

## Candidate ideas to rank
- Return stories with an accessibility tag (S5.5.2, S5.5.3)
- No-trip returns first, with the route named (S5.5.4)
- A draft "accessibility request" message the user can send a store (extends S5.4.5)
- A short "your rights, in general terms" panel with sources
- A per-seller note: "mail return free", "curbside return available", "store visit required"
- Reminders timed so there is room to arrange a ride or help
- App accessibility audit against WCAG 2.2 AA, with real assistive-technology testing (links to S9.1.1)

## Source note
From a scooter seller, as relayed by Steve: while on the scooter you are legally a pedestrian and may go where a person walking can go; where it will not fit is a case-by-case question. This matches the general US rule that mobility scooters designed for people with mobility disabilities count as wheelchairs under the ADA regulations (stores cannot ask about the disability or ban them from pedestrian areas, only act on a legitimate safety limit or a space that cannot physically fit). State pedestrian rules vary. Verify wording against 28 CFR 36.311 and DOJ guidance before putting any of it in the app (see question 3).
